Email Fraud Risk Screening — Two-Layer ML Pipeline

Word + character TF-IDF & Linear SVM (Layer 1) feeding a gradient-boosted screening model (Layer 2) — built after discovering the base SVM, though excellent on its Enron training distribution, didn't generalise to real academic-domain phishing.

93.3%

0.75

How it works, in plain terms: Subject + body text is transformed into a 450,000-dimension word + character TF-IDF representation, scored by a Linear SVM. Because that SVM was trained on Enron corporate email, it under-recognised fraud patterns typical of academic correspondence (predatory journals, fake conference invitations) — so I built and validated a second-layer gradient-boosted model that reinterprets the SVM's own score, tested on an independently collected, hand-labelled academic-domain set. When an initial 5-tier risk system failed a monotonicity check on that validation set, I replaced it with quantile-derived boundaries — split into equal-sized groups by rank and empirically confirmed to produce a monotonically increasing spam rate (30% → 55% → 65%) — rather than shipping thresholds that looked precise but weren't backed by evidence.

Ethical Dilemmas Decision-Making Dataset

100 original ethical dilemmas across 10 real-world domains, each posed to three different AI models to benchmark consistency in moral/ethical reasoning — published on Kaggle with full documentation and citation metadata.

I wrote all 100 scenarios myself — Medical, Law, Education, Examinations, Social, Cultural, Political, Financial, Data Science, and Research, 10 each — with two fixed decision options per dilemma so every model faces an identical choice structure. Each scenario was then run through Claude, Copilot, and Gemini, recording each model's pick and, where offered, its proposed third-way resolution. The result is a lightweight benchmark for inter-model agreement on ethics, not a ground-truth answer key — 10 scenarios do carry an implied baseline answer to sanity-check response quality.

The Model: Gradient-Boosted Ensemble

How it works, in plain terms: three gradient-boosted decision tree models — LightGBM, XGBoost, and CatBoost — were trained independently on engineered ratio and interaction features (screen time splits, social-media-to-total-time ratios, and missingness flags). Each model learns slightly different patterns because of how it builds its trees. Rather than trusting one model alone, I blended their out-of-fold predictions and searched for the best mix — landing on 25% LightGBM + 75% XGBoost (CatBoost added no further lift once blended). Ensembling this way smooths out any single model's blind spots.

Identifying Burnout Severity Clusters

An interactive walkthrough of the clustering approach from one of my manuscripts currently under review: using K-means to split the cohort into available clusters. That is the beauty of clustering; we assign group names later. In this study I found 3 clusters, and in profiling I realized they show features suggestive of low / moderate / high burnout groups. The basis of kmeans clustering was the Oldenburg Burnout Inventory (OLBI) exhaustion and disengagement scores, then deriving cutoff thresholds between clusters.
